A balance sheet is a photograph, not a film. It shows what a business owned and owed on one specific date — which makes it useless on its own and revealing the moment you put two of them side by side.

This is an order of operations. Follow it in sequence and you'll extract most of what a balance sheet has to tell you before you run out of ten minutes.

## Minute 1: check it balances, and note the date

Assets = Liabilities + Equity. If it doesn't balance, stop; you have a data problem, not an analysis problem.

Note the date. A balance sheet dated 31 March in India is a year-end statement, and year-end figures are the most managed figures a company produces. Receivables get chased, payables get delayed, and inventory gets counted carefully — precisely once a year. A 31 March number is not a typical day.

## Minutes 2–3: the current ratio

Current assets ÷ current liabilities.

Above 1.0 means the business can cover the next twelve months of obligations from assets that convert to cash in the same period. Below 1.0 is a warning, though not automatically a crisis — supermarkets run below 1.0 routinely because they collect cash instantly and pay suppliers on credit.

The number matters less than its direction. Compare against the prior year, which is printed in the adjacent column of every published balance sheet. A ratio falling from 1.8 to 1.1 tells you far more than either figure alone.

## Minutes 4–5: what receivables and inventory are doing

These two line items are where trouble shows up first, because both are estimates dressed as facts.

Compare their growth to revenue growth from the P&L:

- Receivables growing faster than revenue means customers are taking longer to pay — or that sales were booked that will never be collected.
- Inventory growing faster than revenue means goods aren't moving. In a business with perishable or fashion-driven stock, that inventory is worth less than the carrying value states.

Both patterns can precede a bad year while the income statement still looks fine. This is the single highest-value check on the page.

## Minutes 6–7: the debt position

Find total borrowings, then split them into current and non-current.

Two questions:

1. **How much is due within a year?** A company with modest total debt but a large repayment falling due next quarter has a liquidity problem the debt-to-equity ratio won't reveal.
2. **How does total debt compare to equity?** Debt-to-equity above 2.0 is high for most sectors and normal for banks and infrastructure. Sector context is mandatory here; there is no universal threshold.

## Minutes 8–9: read the notes on contingent liabilities

Contingent liabilities sit *off* the balance sheet — disputed tax demands, guarantees given for subsidiaries, pending litigation. They're disclosed in the notes rather than the face of the statement.

For Indian companies, disputed tax demands are routinely large and routinely settled for a fraction of the claim. But a contingent liability approaching the size of net worth deserves attention, because the balance sheet you're reading has already excluded it from every ratio you calculated.

## Minute 10: check related-party transactions

Also in the notes. Look for large receivables from or loans to group companies.

Money owed by a related party is technically an asset and practically a question mark — the collection decision sits with people who control both sides. It doesn't imply wrongdoing. It does mean the asset's quality depends on judgement rather than an arm's-length obligation.

## What you have after ten minutes

Not a valuation. But you'll know whether the company can pay its bills, whether its working capital is deteriorating, how much debt is coming due, and what obligations sit outside the statement.

That's usually enough to decide whether the company deserves a longer look — which is the actual purpose of reading a balance sheet quickly.

## Practice

Pull the annual report of any listed Indian company and run these ten minutes on it. Then do the same for a competitor.

The comparison teaches more than either statement alone, because it makes sector norms visible. A debt-to-equity ratio that looks alarming in isolation often turns out to be the sector's median.

Most people meet pivot tables through a colleague who builds one in eight seconds and then can't explain what they did. This walks through the same eight seconds slowly enough that you can repeat them on your own data.

Download the practice file — a 4,000-row retail sales extract with columns for date, region, salesperson, product category, units, and revenue — from the Resources library before you start.

## What a pivot table actually does

A pivot table answers one question: *for each group, what is the total?* Everything else is variation on that.

Your raw data has one row per transaction. That's the wrong shape for almost every question a manager asks. Nobody wants to know about transaction 2,847. They want revenue by region, or units by category by month. A pivot table regroups rows without touching the underlying data — which is why it's safe, and why it's reversible.

The mental model that helps: you are choosing which column becomes the **rows**, which becomes the **columns**, and which numeric column gets **summed** in the middle.

## Building your first one

Click anywhere inside your data, then Insert → PivotTable → OK. Excel drops an empty frame on a new sheet with a field list on the right.

Now drag:

- `Region` into **Rows**
- `Revenue` into **Values**

That's it. You have revenue by region. Four fields sit in that panel — Filters, Columns, Rows, Values — and almost all of your work is deciding what goes where.

Add `Product Category` to **Columns** and the flat list becomes a matrix: regions down the side, categories across the top, revenue in the cells. This is the moment pivot tables usually click.

## The three settings that cause most confusion

**Count instead of Sum.** Drop a numeric field into Values and Excel usually sums it. Drop one with any blank or text cell and it silently switches to Count, which produces numbers that look plausible and are wrong. Click the field in Values → Value Field Settings → Sum. Check this every time a total looks strange.

**Grouping dates.** Drag a date field into Rows and you get one row per day, which is rarely useful. Right-click any date in the pivot → Group → choose Months and Years. Always include Years — grouping by month alone merges January 2025 into January 2026 without warning.

**Percentages.** Drag `Revenue` into Values a second time, then set Show Values As → % of Grand Total. You now have absolute revenue and share of revenue side by side, which is what most reporting requests actually want.

## Refreshing without breaking things

Pivot tables do not update when the source data changes. Right-click → Refresh, or Data → Refresh All.

If your source grows by rows over time, convert it to a table first (`Ctrl+T`) before creating the pivot. A pivot built on a table expands with it. A pivot built on a fixed range like `A1:F4000` will quietly ignore row 4,001 forever, and this is the single most common source of wrong numbers in spreadsheets that used to be right.

## Practice

Using the sales extract, produce:

1. Revenue by region, sorted highest to lowest
2. Units by product category by quarter
3. Each salesperson's revenue as a percentage of their region's total

The third one is the interesting one — it needs Show Values As → % of Parent Row Total, not % of Grand Total. Getting that distinction right is most of what separates a usable report from a misleading one.

## Where this goes next

Once you're comfortable here, the natural next step isn't more Excel — it's understanding *why* the regroup-and-aggregate pattern works, because it's the same operation as `GROUP BY` in SQL and the same operation as a matrix visual in Power BI. Learn it once, in whichever tool is in front of you, and it transfers.
